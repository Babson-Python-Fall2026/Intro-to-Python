Problem Solving Workbook

How to move from understanding a problem to designing a solution a computer can carry out.

Why a method?

When someone describes a problem, we can often imagine what a successful result looks like. But knowing what the result should be does not tell us how to produce it with a program.

Some problems are naturally divided into components with clear inputs and outputs. Others are easy for a person to grasp but surprisingly hard to express as computer operations. Most substantial problems involve both.

Our goal is not to memorize a checklist. It is to develop ways of thinking that help us whenever we reach a point where we do not yet know what to do.

Three challenges in problem solving

Challenge 1 · Step 1

Understand

What does the problem mean? What should happen? What rules and assumptions determine a correct answer?

Challenge 2 · Step 2

Organize

What pieces of work are needed? What goes into each component, what comes out, and what changes?

Challenge 3 · Steps 3–7

Discover an algorithm

How can a computer perform this work using explicit data, operations, decisions, and repetition?

Step 8 brings the components together: integrate, code, and test.

These are not three stages we complete once. Understanding the problem suggests components. Exploring a component's algorithm may reveal that it needs different information or a different decomposition. We revise our earlier decisions as we learn.

Example 1: draw an X

Any person can draw it. The challenge is working out how a computer, which prints text one line at a time, can produce it.

Once we understand how transactions affect cash and shares, the challenge is decomposition: deciding the components and how they work together, which one produces what another needs. Inside each component, we still have to think the way the computer must.

A working method

Use the three challenges as questions to guide your work. Each challenge has numbered steps: concrete moves you can make, including when you get stuck. The numbers give everyone, including the AI instructor, a shared vocabulary: “I'm on step 4” means something specific.

Challenge 1 · Understand the problem

Step 1. Define the problem: begin with a concrete result

  • Describe success precisely. For a calculation or picture: show an input and its exact output. For an interactive system: show an action, its result, and what changes afterward.
  • Find the rules. Use the assignment and the domain itself. If the domain is unfamiliar, work through one small example from beginning to end before designing the system.
  • Expose open questions. What happens if a value is zero, missing, invalid, or at a boundary? If the assignment doesn't settle it, decide it and write it down, rather than letting code decide accidentally.
  • State simplifying assumptions. You may set part of the problem aside for now (only odd sizes, say). Write that down too. It stays on your list until you handle it.

When stuck: If you cannot work out what the answer should be for a tiny example, you do not understand the problem well enough yet. Return to the meaning and the rules.

↓ ↑ The outline is a first guess, not a commitment
Challenge 2 · Organize the solution

Step 2. Decompose and outline the system

Find the components from user actions or meaningful parts of the desired output, and draw a flowchart of how they fit together.

  • Lending desk menu
    • Check out
    • Return
    • Availability report

Example: an equipment lending desk. Students check out calculators and return them. Each user action is a component: Check out takes a student's name and records the loan; Return takes the name and ends the loan; Availability report changes nothing and outputs how many are available and who has one.

For each component, say what goes in, and what comes out or changes: what information its function receives, what it returns, and what it changes in the system. Check out, for example, changes who has a calculator; it doesn't return a result. If you can't say what goes in and what comes out or changes, the component isn't well defined yet.

Identify the categories: the situations a component can face, which depend on its inputs, the system's state, or both. Check out has to handle: a calculator is available and the student doesn't have one; none are available; the student already has one. Each category needs its own handling.

Spanning the universe of possibilities. Together, the categories should span the universe: every possible situation falls into exactly one category. That means they are exhaustive (nothing is left out) and mutually exclusive (nothing falls into two). You may know this from other courses as MECE. In Python, an if / elif / else chain does both: elif makes the branches exclusive, and else catches everything left over. When categories can overlap, the first branch that matches wins, so the order of the checks is a decision, not an accident.

System state. By now you can make a first guess at what the system must know between actions. It comes from the components' effects: if Check out changes who has a calculator, the system must keep track of who has one. For the lending desk, a first guess is the number of calculators available and who has one. It's a guess; the Discover steps may show that part of it can be derived. (Could the number available be computed from who has one?)

The flowchart and each component's inputs, outputs, effects, and categories, with your guess at system state, form the system outline: the basis of your program. Each component becomes a function, and the flowchart shows how they fit together.

When stuck: If a component has no clear responsibility or you cannot say what it receives and produces, revise the outline. If you still lack domain understanding, go back to a worked example.

↓ ↑ Sometimes the algorithm forces a new decomposition
Challenge 3 · Discover the computational solution

Steps 3–7. Work inside one component

  1. Pick the simplest meaningful example. Remove distractions, but keep the difficulty that makes the problem interesting.
  2. Solve it by hand, the way the computer must. First restate the problem in terms of what the computer can do in this setting: read, calculate, compare, repeat, store, or output. If your human solution relies on an operation the computer cannot perform in this setting, find a new representation. Then carry out the small example using only those operations. What you had to remember is your candidate state; the operations you performed are your candidate transitions. Recheck your step 2 guess of system state: did the hand solution need something the guess left out, or did you never use something it included? Then check that your hand solution really produces the result from step 1.
  3. Design the component and draft its pseudocode. From your hand solution, write the component's inputs, state, transitions, invariants, and data representation: your ISTI design. Then write the steps you took by hand as pseudocode.
  4. Generalize and trace. Work a second, different example through your pseudocode. What stays the same is the procedure. Where the draft fails or hard-codes the first example, explain what changed: why did it change, and what does it depend on? The answer becomes part of the algorithm, as an input, a calculation, or a new category. Repeated work suggests a loop; information carried between steps suggests state. Revise the design and the pseudocode until a person could translate it to Python without inventing missing logic, then trace it through both examples.
  5. Add edge cases and revise. Only after the simple case works. Return to your categories: handle each one you set aside, and the boundaries between them. For each, decide whether it's a bug (the code doesn't do what you decided) or an open question (no one decided).

When stuck: If your pseudocode says “calculate holdings” or “draw the X,” it is still naming the task, not explaining how to do it. Make the operation smaller and more concrete.

Steps 3–7 are done for one component at a time. Expect to go back to earlier steps, sometimes all the way to step 2.

Bringing it together

Step 8. Integrate, code, and test

Combine the components and revisit the outline: does each responsibility belong to exactly one component? Decide what is stored (the source of truth) and what is derived. Translate the pseudocode into Python. Your hand examples become your first tests, since you already know the right answers. Add a test for each category.

What students should produce: a worked example with expected result (step 1); a system outline (step 2); a step-by-step procedure in pseudocode (steps 5 and 6); and a trace or test showing that it produces the expected result (steps 6 and 8). These are evidence of reasoning—not paperwork for its own sake.

Example 1: An X inside a square

A person can draw this at a glance. Most of the work in this example is translation: restating what a person does in terms of what a computer can do.

Step 1 · Understand: What is the exact result?

Given size, print a square border made of # characters and an X of X characters joining the inside corners. The size includes the border. Here is the smallest example that shows a real X pattern:

##### #X X# # X # #X X# #####

Input: size = 5. Output: exactly five rows, each with exactly five characters. For now, we make a simplifying assumption: odd sizes of at least 3. Even sizes and invalid sizes are separate cases to revisit later.

Step 2 · Organize: Why the first decomposition fails

A person might say, “Draw a square, then draw two diagonals.” That is an intuitive decomposition, but our program is printing characters to a console. It cannot draw diagonal strokes onto an existing picture. It produces a sequence of characters and lines.

So we change the representation: produce the square one row at a time. On an odd-sized square, every row is exactly one of three row types:

Row typeExampleRule
Border row#####First and last
Two-X row#X X#Interior, but not the middle
One-X row# X #The middle row

For the sizes we are handling, these three row types span the universe of rows: every row belongs to one type, and no row belongs to two. That lets us express the solution with a decision.

The entire system has one component, draw the square. Inside it, the work is now building one row at a time: given the size and a row number, produce the string for that row.

Where the components come from. In many problems, the components come from the problem itself: the user's actions, or the parts of a report. Here they come from the computer. The problem gives us one component, draw the square. What the computer can do (print one line at a time, built left to right) forces us to break it into rows, and each row into segments. Example 2 shows the other pattern: the business rules supply the components, and inside them we still have to think the way the computer must.

The sizes fall into categories too: odd sizes of 3 or more, even sizes, size 1, and size 0 or less. Our assumption sets all but the first aside for now.

Steps 3–4 · Discover: Build one row using allowed operations

The program can construct a string by joining segments. A two-X row consists of seven segments:

#leading spacesXspaces betweenXtrailing spaces#

For the first interior row of the 5×5, those segments have lengths 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1. This produces #X X#. For the middle row, there is only one X: # X #.

Step 5 · Design the component and draft its pseudocode

From the hand solution:

  • Inputs: size.
  • State: which row we're on; its row type; its segment lengths; the row being built.
  • Transitions: build the row from its segments, print it, move on to the next row.
  • Invariants: exactly size rows are printed, each with exactly size characters.
  • Representation: each row is a string.

And a first draft of the pseudocode, written straight from the 5×5:

Draw an X (draft, from the 5×5):
    For each row from 0 through size - 1:
        If the row is the first or the last:
            Print size "#" characters.
        Otherwise, if the row is the middle row (size // 2):
            Print "#", 1 space, "X", 1 space, "#".
        Otherwise:
            Print "#", "X", 1 space, "X", "#".

The draft works for the 5×5. But look at the numbers in it: are they really constants?

Find the pattern before reading further

What is the relationship between the row number and leading spaces in the top half? What do you notice about the bottom half? How can you calculate the spaces between the X's if the whole row must be size characters wide?

Check the rules you discovered

The bottom half repeats the top half in reverse, without repeating the middle. For a two-X row in the top half, leading = row − 1, and trailing spaces equal leading spaces.

A two-X row contains two # characters and two X characters. Its length is therefore 4 + 2 × leading + between = size. Solving for the unknown gives:

between = size - 4 - 2 * leading

For the middle row, there are two # characters, one X, and equal leading and trailing spaces: 3 + 2 × leading = size. Therefore leading = (size − 3) // 2 for odd sizes.

Step 6 (continued) · Revise the design and the pseudocode

We have discovered three building rules, enough to build any top-half row from size and its row number. The bottom half needs no new rules: its rows are the top-half rows again, in reverse, without the middle. For the 5×5, rows 0, 1, 2 are followed by 1, 0. So the bottom loop builds rows exactly as the top loop does; only its range changes, counting down from middle − 1 to 0. In the bottom loop, the loop variable doesn't number the row being printed; it selects which top-half row to print again. It never reaches the middle, so it needs no one-X branch. Because every row is rebuilt from size and its number, we never have to remember earlier rows.

The revised design keeps much less than the first. Working state: which row we are producing, and the string being built. Derived values: middle row, leading spaces, between spaces, and row type. Invariants: unchanged.

Draw an X (odd sizes of at least 3):
    Set middle to size // 2.

        If row is 0:

This is detailed pseudocode: the order, the decisions, the repetitions, and all the calculations are specified. Trace it for size 5: the top loop prints rows 0, 1, 2 and the bottom loop prints 1, 0. That's exactly the five lines in the first picture; size 7 gives rows 0, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 0 and matches the table.

Python translation

Every line of the pseudocode translates directly; the only new choices are Python mechanics, such as range(middle - 1, -1, -1) for “from middle − 1 down through 0.”

def draw_x(size):                                # odd sizes of at least 3
    middle = size // 2

    # Top half: print each row, from the border down to the middle.
    for row in range(middle + 1):
        if row == 0:                             # border
            print('#' * size)
        elif row < middle:                       # two-X row
            leading = row - 1
            between = size - 4 - 2 * leading
            print('#' + ' ' * leading + 'X' + ' ' * between + 'X' + ' ' * leading + '#')
        else:                                    # row is middle: one-X row
            leading = (size - 3) // 2
            print('#' + ' ' * leading + 'X' + ' ' * leading + '#')

    # Bottom half: print the top half's rows again, in reverse, without the middle.
    for top_row in range(middle - 1, -1, -1):    # which top-half row to repeat
        if top_row == 0:                         # border
            print('#' * size)
        else:                                    # two-X row
            leading = top_row - 1
            between = size - 4 - 2 * leading
            print('#' + ' ' * leading + 'X' + ' ' * between + 'X' + ' ' * leading + '#')


draw_x(5)

Choosing string multiplication is a Python mechanic; determining how many spaces belong in each row was the problem-solving work.

Step 7 · Revisit the cases we set aside

Return to the size categories and trace each one through the pseudocode.

  • Size 3: rows 0, 1, 0 give ###, #X#, ###. It works.
  • Size 1: the middle is 0, so row 0 is both the border row and the middle row. The row types overlap. The top loop checks for the border first, so it prints #. But the order of the checks made a decision nobody made on purpose: should a 1×1 square be # or X? That's an open question. Decide it, or declare size 1 invalid.
  • Size 0 or less: size 0 prints one empty line and negative sizes print nothing. These sizes are most likely invalid: reject them.
  • Even sizes: trace size 6. The middle is 3, so the rows are 0, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 0:
###### #X X# # XX # # X # ← shouldn't exist, and it's only 5 wide # XX # #X X# ######

Seven lines instead of six, and one of them is the wrong width. Both invariants catch it. An even square has no middle row: its two center rows are the same two-X row. So the middle-row branch should run only for odd sizes:

        Otherwise, if size is odd:                     (row is middle; only odd sizes have one)
            Set leading to (size - 3) // 2.
            Print "#", leading spaces, "X", leading spaces, "#".
        (Even sizes have no middle row, so nothing prints for row middle.)

With size 6, the top loop prints rows 0, 1, 2 and skips row 3; the bottom loop prints 2, 1, 0. Six lines, with a 2×2 center.

Step 8 · Code and test

There's only one component, so integrating means checking that the outline still holds: the size goes in, size lines come out, and nothing is kept. The only changes from step 6 are the even-size fix to the middle-row branch and a check that rejects sizes below 1. Then test one size from each category, starting with the 5×5 you worked by hand.

The comment after the last elif matters. An elif with no else is fine only when the remaining cases need nothing; the comment shows that was decided, not forgotten.

What this example teaches: the important move was not breaking the picture into a border and diagonals. It was finding a representation compatible with the output operation: rows, then segments within rows. The draft from one example hard-coded its numbers; the second example showed what they depend on. The first design kept five things; the final design keeps two.

Example 2: A tiny portfolio

Here the work is mostly understanding the rules and decomposing the system—but we still have to invent algorithms inside the components.

Step 1 · Understand: Work one financial example end to end

Imagine a tiny version of PEMS with one client, a cash security named $$$$, and one stock named ABC. Every transaction record has the same fields: trans_id, trd_day (the trading day), ticker, trn_typ (the type), shares, cps (cost per share), and total_cost. Cash is represented like any other security; with a cps of 1, its shares are dollars.

Our example has these closing prices:

trd_day123
ABC close$150$155$160

On day 1 the client contributes $1,000. On day 2 the client buys 5 ABC shares. The purchase uses day 1's $150 close, so it costs $750. Buying a stock means paying for it with cash, so we record it as two transaction records:

trd_daytrn_typtickersharescpstotal_cost
1CONTRIBUTION$$$$1,00011,000
2BUYABC5150750
2SELL$$$$7501750

At the end of day 2, the client holds $$$$ 250 and ABC 5 shares. The day-2 report uses the day-2 closing price: 5 × $155 + $250 = $1,025. A report as of day 1 shows just $$$$ 1,000: a report includes only the transactions up to its day. We can produce the right answer by hand before discussing Python.

The rules this example uses:

  • Cash is a security, $$$$, with a cps of 1.
  • Every stock trade is saved with its cash leg, immediately after it: a stock BUY with a $$$$ SELL, a stock SELL with a $$$$ BUY.
  • Prices: a trade executes at the close of trd_day − 1; a report values holdings at the close of the report day.
  • Cash and shares can never go negative. A transaction that would break a rule is rejected, and nothing is recorded.
  • Average cost: a sale removes cost at the shares sold × (total cost ÷ shares held), not at the sale's proceeds.
  • Saved transactions can be trusted: each was checked when it was recorded.
The full PEMS rules
  1. Cash is the security $$$$, with a cps of 1. Money is rounded to 2 decimal places.
  2. CONTRIBUTION and WITHDRAWAL apply only to $$$$.
  3. Stock tickers come from a ticker universe list, and every listed ticker has a closing price for every day from 0 to 99. A ticker not on the list is rejected. Stock shares are whole numbers.
  4. Every stock trade is saved with its cash leg, immediately after it: a stock BUY with a $$$$ SELL, a stock SELL with a $$$$ BUY. With no corporate actions (rule 13), cash is never bought or sold on its own.
  5. Prices: a trade executes at the closing price of trd_day − 1. The system computes cps and total_cost (shares × cps). A report values holdings at the closing price of the report day, which must be between 1 and the current trd_day.
  6. Cash can never be negative: a BUY or a WITHDRAWAL needs enough cash.
  7. A client can't sell shares they don't own: shares can never be negative. A transaction's shares must be positive; 0 means no trade, and nothing is recorded.
  8. A transaction that would break a rule is rejected, not recorded. Recorded transactions can't be edited or deleted.
  9. Time: trd_day runs from 1 to 99 and only moves forward, through adv_trd_day, which stops at 99. New transactions are recorded only on the current day, and a client can make many on one day.
  10. Clients: every client's first transaction is their opening cash contribution, recorded when the client was created. Only an existing client can be selected.
  11. Cost: a sale reduces a position's total cost at the average cost: the shares sold × (total cost ÷ shares held).
  12. Simplifying assumption: the saved transactions are never changed outside the system. Every saved transaction was checked when it was recorded, so when we read them back in, we can trust them.
  13. Simplifying assumption: no corporate actions. Dividends and splits with cash in lieu would create a $$$$ BUY with no stock trade before it, so rule 4 and the cash invariants hold only under this assumption.

What the rules imply. Rules don't just constrain the program; they tell you what it needs.

  • Recording a transaction needs the client's current holdings: you can't check cash or shares without them.
  • A report for any day from 1 to today needs the holdings as of that day, so holdings must exist for every day, not just today.
  • Only the current day's holdings can change; earlier days are fixed.
  • Holdings come from the transactions, which are the source of truth.
  • Holdings start empty. Day 0 has no holdings; $$$$ appears with the opening contribution and stays, even at 0. Any other ticker that drops to 0 shares is removed.

Before designing the program

Which facts came from finance (for example, buying stock requires payment), and which were our choices about representation (for example, recording a stock trade and its cash leg as two records)? Which of these could a designer change, and what else would have to change with it?

Step 2 · Organize: Outline the system

For a system that responds to actions, decomposition starts from what the user can do. Here is the PEMS menu structure, defined in menus.json:

The transaction actions face many categories: enough cash or not, enough shares or not, a ticker that isn't in the universe, a quantity of 0, and more. Several can apply at once. In Part B, an if / elif / else chain handles them: covering every possibility makes the chain exhaustive, and elif makes it mutually exclusive. The order of the checks still matters. It keeps the program from crashing (we can't look up the price of a ticker that isn't in the universe), and it decides which rejection a request gets when more than one applies.

System state. A first guess at what the system keeps between actions: the clients, their transactions, and their holdings by day, all saved to the file.

But is that really our best guess? Check it against the rules. Holdings come from the transactions, so they're derived. If we saved both, they could disagree. Holdings are still system state: the system has to remember them from one action to the next. They just don't need to be saved. So the file holds only the clients and their transactions. When a client is selected, the holdings are rebuilt and kept in memory, and the transaction actions update them in real time.

We work through these in two parts.

Part A, steps 3–4 · Select client: build holdings from saved transactions, by hand

Two kinds of transactions. A client's transactions come from two places, and the system treats them differently.

  • Saved transactions come from the JSON file. When a client is selected, we apply them in order to build the holdings for every day through today. By the rules, each one was checked when it was recorded, so there's nothing to check now. We just apply them.
  • New transactions are the ones the client makes today. Each must be checked against the current holdings (is there enough cash? enough shares?) before it's saved and applied.

Part A handles saved transactions. Part B handles new ones.

Start with the two-day example. Day 0 has nothing. A new day's holdings begin as a copy of the previous day's; then we apply that day's transaction records in order.

Part A, step 5 · Design the component and draft its pseudocode

  • Inputs: a client's saved transactions and the current trd_day.
  • State: each ticker's shares and total cost, for each day from 0 through the current day.
  • Transitions: start a new day from yesterday's holdings; apply each record to one ticker.
  • Invariants: no ticker's shares, cash included, go negative; only the current day changes.
  • Representation: a dictionary keyed by day, where each day is a dictionary of ticker → position.
Load a client's saved transactions (draft, from days 1–2):
    Start with empty holdings for day 0.
    For each day from 1 through the current day:
        Start from a copy of yesterday's holdings.
        For each saved record for this day, in order:
            If the type is CONTRIBUTION or BUY:
                Add its shares and total_cost to the ticker's position.
            Otherwise (a SELL):
                Subtract its shares and total_cost from the ticker's position.

The draft works for days 1 and 2. The only SELL so far is a cash SELL, where subtracting total_cost happens to be right.

The draft gets cash and shares right, but ABC's cost comes out 750 − 310 = $440: it subtracts the sale's proceeds, not its cost.

Two things changed, and both need explaining. A stock sale must remove cost at the average cost, not at the proceeds; the draft only worked because its one SELL was cash. WITHDRAWAL has no branch of its own: it lands in the SELL branch and works only by accident, and a misspelled type would land there too. The draft's categories didn't span the universe.

The rest of the draft holds up: each day starts from yesterday's holdings, and each record changes one ticker. We must carry forward each ticker's shares and total cost. A dictionary of positions for each day represents that:

position_history = {
    0: {},
    1: {'$$$$': {'shares': 1000, 'total_cost': 1000}},
    2: {'$$$$': {'shares': 250, 'total_cost': 250},
        'ABC':  {'shares': 5,   'total_cost': 750}},
    3: {'$$$$': {'shares': 500, 'total_cost': 500},
        'ABC':  {'shares': 3,   'total_cost': 450}},
}
current_holdings = position_history[3]

Here position_history[3]['ABC']['shares'] is 3. We need a separate copy of each day's positions so that changing today's holdings does not change yesterday's. The revised pseudocode splits the repeated work into smaller algorithms:

Start a new day:
    Copy yesterday's holdings, including a separate copy of every position.

Apply one transaction record to today's holdings:
    Find the ticker's position, or start a new position with 0 shares and 0 cost.
    If the type is CONTRIBUTION or BUY:
        Add the record's shares and total_cost to the position.
    Otherwise, if the type is WITHDRAWAL or SELL:
        Remove shares * (position total cost / position shares) from the position's cost.
        Subtract the record's shares from the position.
    Otherwise:
        Stop: unknown transaction type.
    Round the shares and cost to 2 decimal places.
    If shares are now 0, remove the ticker unless it is $$$$.
    Otherwise, store the updated position.

Load a client's saved transactions:
    Start with empty holdings for day 0.
    For each day from 1 through the current day:
        Start a new day from yesterday's holdings.
        For each saved record for this day, in order:
            Apply that transaction record.
        Keep this day's resulting holdings in position_history.

Trace both examples. Days 1 and 2 give $$$$ 250 and ABC 5 (cost 750), as before. On day 3, the sale removes 2 × (750 ÷ 5) = $300 of cost, leaving ABC 3 (cost 450), and cash ends at $500. Both match the hand calculations.

The important algorithmic insight is not “use a dictionary.” It is process transactions in order, carry positions forward, and apply one general update rule to each record. The dictionary makes that procedure representable.

One invariant deserves attention: every stock BUY is immediately followed by its $$$$ SELL, and every stock SELL by its $$$$ BUY. Why immediately? A check that only looks for some matching cash record on the same day would accept two $100 purchases paired with a single $100 sale, because both purchases find the same sale.

All the invariants
  • CONTRIBUTION and WITHDRAWAL use only $$$$.
  • Every stock BUY is immediately followed by a $$$$ SELL on the same trd_day whose shares equal the stock's total_cost; every stock SELL is immediately followed by a matching $$$$ BUY.
  • With no corporate actions, a $$$$ BUY or SELL only ever appears immediately after a stock trade.
  • total_cost always equals shares × cps. PEMS stores this derived value, so the rule that keeps it consistent is an invariant.
  • A client's first transaction is a CONTRIBUTION.
  • No ticker's shares, cash included, are ever negative, on any day.
  • Every day from 0 through the current trd_day has holdings. Day 0 is empty. From the opening contribution on, $$$$ is always present, even at 0; no other ticker is kept at 0 shares.
  • Only the current day's holdings ever change, and each day's holdings equal what the transactions through that day produce.

Part A, step 7 · Edge cases

  • Selling a whole position: ABC drops to 0 shares and is removed. If cash drops to 0, $$$$ stays, at 0.
  • A client who last traded on day 9 when the current day is 12: loading walks every day through 12, so days 10–12 start from copies of day 9, and current_holdings exists for day 12.
  • A purchase saved without its cash SELL: cash would be overstated. The immediately-followed invariant catches it.

Part A, step 8 · Code and test

Python: implementing “apply” and “load”

Saved transactions were validated when recorded, so the replay function can trust them. Each transaction dictionary uses the PEMS fields; other metadata is omitted here.

import copy


def apply_transaction(holdings, transaction):
    ticker = transaction['ticker']
    position = holdings.get(ticker, {'shares': 0, 'total_cost': 0})
    transaction_type = transaction['trn_typ']
    shares = transaction['shares']

    if transaction_type in ('CONTRIBUTION', 'BUY'):
        position['shares'] += shares
        position['total_cost'] += transaction['total_cost']
    elif transaction_type in ('WITHDRAWAL', 'SELL'):
        average_cost = position['total_cost'] / position['shares']
        position['total_cost'] -= shares * average_cost
        position['shares'] -= shares
    else:
        raise ValueError('Unknown transaction type')

    position['shares'] = round(position['shares'], 2)
    position['total_cost'] = round(position['total_cost'], 2)
    if ticker != '$$$$' and position['shares'] == 0:
        holdings.pop(ticker, None)
    else:
        holdings[ticker] = position


def load_client(transactions, current_day):
    position_history = {0: {}}
    for day in range(1, current_day + 1):
        # start the day from a full copy of yesterday, every position included
        holdings = copy.deepcopy(position_history[day - 1])
        for transaction in transactions:
            if transaction['trd_day'] == day:
                apply_transaction(holdings, transaction)
        position_history[day] = holdings
    return position_history

Two Python details worth noticing: dict.get does not insert a missing ticker; holdings[ticker] = position does that. And a shallow copy (.copy()) of yesterday's holdings would copy only the outer dictionary; the position dictionaries inside would be shared, so changing today's ABC would change yesterday's too. copy.deepcopy copies every position as well.

Select client runs load_client for the chosen client. Test it with the examples you already worked: loading the first example on day 2 should give $$$$ 250 and ABC 5 (cost 750); loading the second on day 3 should give $$$$ 500 and ABC 3 (cost 450).

Part B, step 5 · Design the components and draft their pseudocode

  • Inputs: the request (a ticker and shares, or an amount), current_holdings, and prices.
  • State: current_holdings, updated after each accepted transaction.
  • Transitions: an accepted request adds records and applies them to current_holdings; a rejected one changes nothing.
  • Invariants: cash and shares never go negative.
Buy or Sell (draft, from day 4):
    Set cps to the ticker's close on trd_day - 1, and total_cost to shares * cps.
    Buy: if total_cost is more than the cash in current_holdings, reject it.
    Sell: if the client holds fewer shares than requested, reject it.
    Otherwise, save the record and its cash leg, and apply both to current_holdings.

Part B, step 6 · Generalize and trace

Try requests the day-4 example didn't include: a ticker that isn't in the universe, 0 shares, −3 shares, 2.5 shares of a stock, a withdrawal larger than the cash. The draft handles none of them. Several can be wrong at once (an unknown ticker and not enough cash), so each action handles them with one if / elif / else chain. Checking every possibility makes the chain exhaustive, and elif makes it mutually exclusive. The order still matters: it keeps the program from crashing (we can't look up the price of a ticker that isn't in the universe), and it decides which rejection a request gets when more than one applies.

Every rejection records nothing and reports why.

Check the quantity (shares for Buy or Sell, the amount for Contribution or Withdrawal):
    If it is negative: reject it.
    Otherwise, if it is 0: there's no trade; record nothing.

Buy or Sell (ticker, shares):
    Check the quantity; stop if it rejected or recorded nothing.
    If ticker isn't in the ticker universe: reject it.
        ($$$$ isn't in the universe, so cash can't be bought or sold on its own.)
    Otherwise, if shares isn't a whole number: reject it.
    Otherwise:
        Set cps to ticker's close on trd_day - 1.
        Set total_cost to shares * cps, rounded to 2 decimal places.
        Buy: if total_cost is more than the $$$$ shares in current_holdings,
          reject it (not enough cash).
        Sell: if current_holdings has fewer than shares of ticker (0 if not held),
          reject it (not enough shares).
        Otherwise, record the stock record, then its $$$$ cash leg of total_cost
          (a $$$$ SELL for a Buy, a $$$$ BUY for a Sell).

Contribution or Withdrawal (amount):
    Check the quantity; stop if it rejected or recorded nothing.
    Withdrawal: if amount is more than the $$$$ shares in current_holdings,
      reject it (not enough cash).
    Otherwise, record it with ticker $$$$ and cps 1.

Record (one or more new records):
    Save them with the client's transactions.
    Apply each to current_holdings, in order, as in Part A.
    Report success.

Advance the trading day:
    If trd_day is 99, refuse.
    Otherwise, add 1 to trd_day. (The next time a client is selected,
      their holdings are rebuilt through the new day.)

Trace the day-4 trades: the first BUY fails the cash check; the SELL passes and records ABC and its $$$$ BUY; the second BUY passes against the updated cash. Notice the reuse: the transaction actions use the same “apply one transaction” algorithm as Part A.

Part B, step 7 · Edge cases

  • Several trades on one day: each is checked against current_holdings as it stands after the trades before it, as the day-4 example shows.
  • Selling a whole position: the ticker is removed from current_holdings, so a second sale of it that day is rejected.
  • Advancing past day 99: refused.

Step 8 · Integrate: What is stored, and what is derived?

Working out the algorithms confirms the step 2 decision: saved transactions are the source of truth, and position_history is rebuilt from them. For speed, we keep it in memory while working with the client, but we never save it as a second, independent record.

A useful integration test is to record the day-4 transactions, note the current holdings, and then reload the client from saved transactions. The holdings reconstructed for day 4 must be identical: $$$$ 20 and ABC 6 shares, with remaining cost $940. If they don't match, recording and loading disagree about what a transaction does.

Revising the design

A first design is a hypothesis. We make it so we have something concrete to test; we do not expect it to be perfect. Examples, pseudocode, and tests tell us when to change it.

Missing
Does an operation need information the component does not receive or remember? Add it or change the interface.
Unnecessary
Is information stored but never needed? Consider removing it.
Derived
Could information be recomputed from something already stored? Consider deriving it instead.
Conflict
Could two pieces of stored information disagree? Choose a source of truth, or state how consistency is maintained.

Both examples revised their initial design. In the X, we changed from diagonal strokes to rows and discovered that previous rows need not be stored. In the portfolio, we found that daily holdings could be derived from transaction records, so they need not be saved separately.

When you are stuck, ask: Do I misunderstand the problem, have I organized the work poorly, or have I not yet found a procedure the computer can execute?

The answer tells you which kind of thinking to return to. That is the method.
