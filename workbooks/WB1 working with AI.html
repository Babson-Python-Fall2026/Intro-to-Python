Using AI
Effectively

AI doesn't just answer questions — it opens doors. Whether you walk through them depends entirely on how curious you are.

Why This Workbook Exists

You already know how to use AI. You type a question, you get an answer. That part is easy. What this workbook is about is something harder and more valuable: learning how to think with AI, not just query it.

There is a meaningful difference between a student who pastes a problem into ChatGPT and copies the output, and a student who uses AI the way a good researcher uses a library — as a starting point, a sounding board, and a tool for going deeper. This workbook is designed to help you become the second kind of student.

Who this is for

This workbook is written for students in Computational Thinking with AI and Python. You are juniors and seniors capable of sophisticated thinking. The goal is to make sure AI amplifies that thinking rather than replacing it.

We cover four platforms — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and GitHub Copilot — and show you how to use each for different kinds of tasks. But before any of that, we need to establish the mindset. Because the mindset is everything.

The Three Ways to Ask AI for Help

Every AI conversation about a problem — in this course or anywhere — takes one of three basic shapes. They differ in one thing only: how much of the thinking you keep. Read all three carefully, because the difference between them is the difference between this course working for you or being wasted on you.

Here is the same homework problem — "given a list of students with scores, report how many passed" — asked all three ways.

Approach 1

Outsource the Answer

Never for graded work

You paste the homework problem and copy what comes back.

"Here's my homework problem: [pastes the problem]. Solve it."

You get working code in ten seconds, and you learn nothing. You can't explain it, can't modify it, can't reproduce it on an exam — and you've handed in someone else's thinking with your name on it. For graded work in this course, this approach is an academic integrity violation. But the deeper problem isn't the rule: it's that you paid tuition to practice a skill and then paid an AI to keep you from practicing it.

Approach 2

Outsource the Design

Not for this course

You state the requirements — clearly, even professionally — but leave the computational thinking and design to the AI.

"Write a Python program that takes student records with names and
scores, uses a passing threshold of 60, and reports how many passed
and the percentage. Make it clean and readable."

This is a real skill, and plenty of working professionals will use AI this way for routine tasks. But notice what happened: the AI decided how to represent the students, what the loop looks like, how the count accumulates, and what happens in edge cases you never mentioned, such as an empty list or a score of exactly 60.

Those are not just programming details. They are thinking details.

This course is not mainly about producing programmers. It is about teaching students how to think carefully: how to define a problem, choose a representation, break a task into steps, handle ambiguity, test assumptions, and recognize edge cases. When AI makes all of those decisions, it may produce working software, but it does not build the habits of mind the course is designed to teach.

So the question is not whether students may use AI. They will, and they should learn to use it well. The question is whether they are still doing the thinking - or merely accepting the thinking done for them.

Approach 3

Own the Design — AI as Instructor

Required in this course

You do the computational thinking and design yourself. The AI's job is to be your instructor: sharpening your thinking, exploring alternatives with you, and probing the edge cases you missed.

"I'm working on counting how many students passed. Here's my design:
each student is a dict with 'name' and 'score'; all of them in a
list. I'll loop with a counter, adding 1 when score >= 60.

Before any code: what edge cases am I missing? Is a score of
exactly 60 a pass under my rule? And is there a different way to
represent this data I should consider — what would the tradeoff be?"

Look at who's doing what. The representation, the pattern, and the rule are yours. The AI is interrogating the design, surfacing the boundary case (the >= was your call — did you make it on purpose?), and offering alternatives for you to judge. When code finally gets written, you can predict it before you read it — because you designed it. This is the only approach used for graded work in this course, and the three course prompts in Part 4 exist to hold you to it.

The one-line version

Approach 1 outsources the answer. Approach 2 outsources the design. Approach 3 outsources nothing — it uses AI to make your own thinking better. As you move from 1 to 3, what you hand over shrinks and what you keep grows. The skill this course grades is what you keep.

How the course enforces Approach 3

The three prompts in your OIM3600 project — Explorer, Designer, Architect — are Approach 3 at increasing levels of sophistication, matched to what you've learned. Prompt 1 builds the questioning habits. Prompt 2 adds the data-representation decision. Prompt 3 adds the full ISTI design method. Part 4 of this workbook covers all three and tells you exactly when each one becomes your working prompt.

Be honest with yourself: which of the three approaches describes how you've used AI for schoolwork up to now? What did you get out of those interactions a week later — could you still do the task without the AI?

The Philosophy: Curiosity and the Detective Mindset

Think about how a good detective works. Sherlock Holmes doesn't accept the first explanation he hears. He asks why. He looks for inconsistencies. He follows threads that seem unimportant until they reveal something crucial. He holds the objective clearly in mind even as new information arrives.

"When you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth."

— Arthur Conan Doyle, The Sign of Four

That is the posture to bring to every AI interaction. Not passive acceptance — active interrogation. Not "give me the answer" — "help me understand this, and then let me push on it."

AI gets tunnel vision too

AI systems tend to commit to the first viable approach they find and then elaborate on it. They can lose sight of your original objective as the conversation grows longer. They often produce solutions that are more complex than necessary — because elaborate answers can look more thorough, even when they aren't.

This means you need to be the one who holds the objective. Is this actually solving the right problem? Is there a simpler way? What am I missing?

The contractor analogy

Hiring an AI without staying engaged is like hiring a contractor and walking away. You might come back to a full kitchen renovation when all you needed was a new faucet. The AI does not know your real constraints — your course level, your time, your actual goal. You do. Stay in charge of the problem definition.

Ask an AI tool to help you with a small Python problem. Once it gives you a solution, follow up with: "Is there a simpler beginner-level way to do this?"

What changed? Did the second answer surprise you?

The habit that changes everything

Every AI answer should prompt at least one follow-up question. Not because the answer is necessarily wrong — but because the answer is never the end of the story. The follow-up is where the real learning happens. This is a learnable habit, and by the end of this course it should be automatic.

What AI Is Good At — and Where It Falls Short

Before you rely on AI for any task, it helps to know what it handles well and where it tends to mislead you. This is not a reason to avoid AI — it is a reason to use it deliberately.

AI is strong at
  • Explaining ideas multiple ways
  • Generating examples and analogies
  • Helping brainstorm approaches
  • Checking code structure and syntax
  • Summarizing concepts quickly
  • Walking through reasoning step by step
  • Suggesting alternatives when asked
AI is risky for
  • Facts requiring current verification
  • Accurate citations and sources
  • Your specific course requirements
  • Subtle logic bugs in longer programs
  • Knowing when a solution is too advanced
  • Remembering your full conversation history
  • Knowing when to stop adding complexity
Important

AI answers are confident by design — the model does not hedge the way a careful person would. A wrong answer and a right answer often look identical. Your job is to verify, push back, and ask the AI to show its reasoning.

Think of a subject you know well — something outside of programming. Imagine asking an AI a detailed question about it. Would you be able to tell if the answer was wrong? Now think about what that means for topics where you are the student, not the expert.

Five Principles for Working with AI

These principles apply regardless of platform or task. Return to them whenever an AI interaction feels like it is going sideways.

Principle 01

Be Curious

Never accept the first answer as final. Ask why it works, what it connects to, and what would change if you modified one assumption.

Principle 02

Be a Detective

Follow the clues. If something doesn't make sense, pursue it. If an explanation is vague, ask for specifics. Good detectives do not let things slide.

Principle 03

Hold the Objective

AI can drift. As the conversation grows, the original goal can get lost. Keep pulling the conversation back to what you actually need.

Principle 04

Demand Simplicity

AI defaults to complexity. Always ask: is there a simpler way? What is the minimum needed to solve this? Complexity is not the same as quality.

Principle 05

Verify the Answer

AI can be wrong, incomplete, or confidently mistaken. Ask it to explain its reasoning. Cross-check anything important before relying on it.

Weak vs. Strong AI Use: A Side-by-Side Example

The same problem. Two very different conversations. Notice what changes — and what it means for what you actually learn.

Weak use of AI
Student
Write me a Python function that finds the largest number in a list.
AI
Here is the function: def find_largest(nums): return max(nums)
Student
Thanks.
Strong use of AI
Student
I want to find the largest number in a list. I think I need a loop that tracks a running maximum. Can you ask me questions before writing any code?
AI
Good start. What should happen if the list is empty? And what will your initial value for the running maximum be?
Student
I'll return None for an empty list. I'll set the max to the first element, then loop from index 1.
AI
Solid design. Now — is there an even simpler beginner solution that doesn't require a loop at all?
Student
Wait — does Python have a built-in for this?
What's different

In the weak example, the student accepts a correct-looking answer without understanding it. The AI also wraps a simple built-in function inside another function — which makes the solution look more advanced than it needs to be. For this task, the simplest beginner-level solution is just:

numbers = [4, 9, 2, 15, 7]
largest = max(numbers)
print(largest)

A stronger student asks what each part means, what the function wrapper adds, and whether there is a simpler beginner-level version. (Functions absolutely earn their place once code is reused or organized into a larger program — you'll write many in this course. The point here is narrower: know what every layer of a solution is for, and don't accept layers you can't justify.) The goal is not just to get working code — it is to understand the simplest correct solution.

Take the weak example above and continue it. Ask the AI these four follow-up questions in order:

  1. What does def find_largest(nums) mean?
  2. What does max() do?
  3. Do we actually need to write a separate function here, or is there a simpler way?
  4. Bonus: Rewrite this without the function. What does the simpler version look like?

The Detective Toolkit

These are specific questions to append to any AI interaction. They turn a single exchange into a real investigation. Bookmark this page and return to it often — especially when you feel stuck or when an answer seems too easy.

Questions to Ask After Any Answer

Go Deeper
  • Why does this work? Walk me through the reasoning.
  • What are the edge cases or exceptions?
  • How does this connect to [concept from class]?
  • What would break this solution?
Challenge the Approach
  • Is there a simpler way to do this?
  • What are two or three completely different approaches?
  • Are you overcomplicating this?
  • What is the minimum needed to solve the original problem?
  • What approach would you NOT recommend, and why?
Reconnect to the Objective
  • Step back — does this actually solve the original problem?
  • What parts of this are essential vs. nice to have?
  • If you had to cut this in half, what would you remove?
  • Am I asking the right question, or is there a better framing?
Force Simplicity
  • Show me the simplest possible version of this solution.
  • What is the core idea, stripped of everything extra?
  • Explain this like I have never seen it before.
Verify the Answer
  • How confident are you in this? What could make you wrong?
  • Show me a case where this would not work.
  • Is this consistent with what you said earlier?
Run It — the Programmer's Superpower
  • Before running any AI code: predict what it will output. Then run it and compare.
  • Test the edges: the empty list, the zero, the boundary value. Does it still behave?
  • Break it on purpose: change one input and predict the new output before running.
  • If the output surprises you, that surprise IS the lesson — chase it.
The interpreter is the ultimate fact-checker

Most fields have no fast way to verify what an AI claims. Programming does: run the code. Every claim an AI makes about Python can be tested in seconds on your own machine — and the habit of predicting the output before you run is the single fastest way to find out whether the understanding in your head matches reality. Use this superpower constantly. It costs nothing and it never lies.

Remember

The detective toolkit is not a checklist to run through every time. It is a set of instincts you are building. Over time, these questions should become automatic — the way a good editor automatically asks "is every word earning its place?"

Part 3 · Question Types

How to Frame
Your Question

AI gives you what you ask for — not what you need. The difference is in how you frame the question.

This Section Is About General AI Literacy

Parts 2 and 4 of this workbook cover how to use AI specifically for this course — the OIM3600 project, the three course prompts. That is course-specific guidance tied to Python, your labs, and your grade.

This section is different. It has nothing to do with the course prompts. It is about a skill you will use for the rest of your life: knowing how to frame a question so AI gives you what you actually need.

The core problem

AI is not a search engine. It does not return a fixed answer to a fixed query. It responds to the shape of your question. Ask a vague question, get a vague answer. Ask a specific question with a clear statement of what you want, get something genuinely useful. The seven types in this section cover the most common question shapes — and how to get each one right.

These patterns work with any AI platform — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot — on any topic, in any course, in any job. They are not course requirements. They are habits worth building.

All Seven Question Types

Listed in order of how commonly you will need them — starting with the ones most immediately useful for any technical work, ending with the broader skills.

1

Debugging & Diagnosis

Something is broken. Find and explain what went wrong.

2

Learn & Understand

You have an answer but you want to understand it deeply.

3

Outcome-Only

You just need it done. You don't need to understand the internals.

4

Guided Problem-Solving

You want to solve it yourself with AI asking questions, not giving answers.

5

Iterative Refinement

You have something that works. You want to make it better.

6

Research & Synthesis

You need to understand a topic, compare options, or summarize a concept.

7

Creative & Generative

You want ideas, options, or original content rather than a fixed answer.

The most important rule for all seven types

Tell Claude what kind of answer you want before you ask the question. "Here is my broken code" gives Claude nothing to work with. "Here is my broken code — I want to understand what went wrong, not just get a fix" gives Claude everything it needs to respond usefully.

The Seven Types in Detail

Type 1

Debugging & Diagnosis

Goal: understand what went wrong and why — not just get a fix

Debugging is the first thing students need AI for and the easiest to do badly. The weak approach is pasting broken code and waiting. The strong approach gives Claude everything it needs: the code, the error, what you expected, and what you want from the response.

Critically — always say whether you want an explanation, a hint, or a fix. Without that, Claude will default to fixing it for you, which teaches you nothing.

  • Always paste the exact error message, not a paraphrase of it
  • Describe what you expected the code to do vs. what it actually did
  • Say explicitly: "explain what went wrong" vs. "give me a hint" vs. "fix it"
  • After getting the explanation, ask: could this error happen anywhere else in my code?
Framing pattern 
Here is my Python code and the error I am getting.

[PASTE YOUR CODE]

Error message: [PASTE EXACT ERROR]

I expected this code to [WHAT YOU THOUGHT WOULD HAPPEN].
Instead it [WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED].

Do not fix it yet. First explain what went wrong and why.
Then ask me if I want a hint or the fix.
Weak framing
My code doesn't work. Can you fix it?

[pastes code]
Claude fixes the code. Student copies it. Nothing learned.
Strong framing
Here is my code. I'm getting a KeyError on line 8. I expected the loop to find each ticker in the dictionary but it seems to be missing one. Don't fix it yet — explain what a KeyError means and what usually causes it in a loop like this.
Claude explains. Student understands. Student can now fix it and spot the same error next time.

Find a bug in your current code — or introduce one deliberately. Use the framing pattern above and ask Claude to explain rather than fix. Write down what you learned that you would not have learned from just getting the fix.

Type 2

Learn & Understand

Goal: build a mental model, not just get a working answer

This type is for when you have code that works — or a concept explained — but you don't really understand it yet. The failure mode is accepting explanations that are technically correct but too abstract to be useful.

The key framing moves are: ask for an analogy, ask for a concrete example, and ask Claude to connect the concept to something you already know. "Explain recursion" gets a textbook answer. "Explain recursion using the portfolio system as an example" gets something you can hold onto.

  • Ask for an analogy or real-world example, not just a definition
  • Ask Claude to connect the concept to code you have already written
  • After the explanation, ask: what would break this? What is the edge case?
  • Ask: explain this like I have never programmed before, then build up from there
Framing pattern 
I want to understand [CONCEPT], not just use it.

Don't give me a textbook definition. Instead:
1. Give me a concrete example using [RELEVANT CONTEXT]
2. Show me what it looks like in simple Python code
3. Then ask me to explain it back to you in my own words

Start with the simplest possible version.
Weak framing
What is a dictionary in Python?
Gets a definition. Student reads it, forgets it, moves on.
Strong framing
I want to understand dictionaries deeply, not just know what they are. Give me a concrete real-world analogy, then show me a simple example in Python. After that, ask me to explain back to you when I would choose a dictionary over a list.
Gets a meaningful explanation with a memorable analogy. Student has to explain it back, which confirms understanding.

Pick a concept you understand how to use but could not confidently explain to someone else — in any subject, not just Python. Use this framing pattern and ask AI to build your mental model, not just show you syntax or definitions.

Type 3

Outcome-Only

Goal: get it done — understanding the internals is not required

Not every problem requires deep understanding. If you need to connect two computers on the same network, configure a VS Code setting, or install a package, you just need it to work. This is a legitimate use of AI — and the framing is different from every other type.

For outcome-only questions: be specific about your environment, state exactly what "done" looks like, and skip the explanation request. The failure mode here is the opposite of the others — getting too much explanation when you just needed the steps.

  • State your operating system and environment upfront — it changes the answer
  • Describe exactly what success looks like: "I can ping machine B from machine A"
  • Ask for the simplest solution — AI defaults to comprehensive, you want minimal
  • If the solution seems too complex, ask: is there a simpler way to achieve just this goal?
Framing pattern 
I need to [SPECIFIC GOAL]. I don't need to understand how it works —
I just need it to work.

My setup: [OS, environment, relevant details]
Success looks like: [EXACTLY WHAT DONE MEANS]

Give me the simplest possible steps. Skip the background explanation.
Where Outcome-Only is off limits

Outcome-Only is for plumbing: network settings, installs, VS Code configuration, getting a tool to run. It is never the right type for lab problems, homework, or exam work — there, the thinking is the deliverable, and Outcome-Only is Approach 1 from Part 1 wearing a respectable coat. If the task is graded, you are in Approach 3 territory and one of the course prompts should be active.

Weak framing
How do I share files between two computers?
Gets a 500-word overview of five different methods. Student has to figure out which one applies.
Strong framing
I need to share a folder between two Windows 11 laptops on the same home WiFi network. I don't need to understand networking — I just need to be able to drag files between them. Give me the simplest steps, skip the explanation.
Gets a direct, minimal answer for exactly that setup. Done in minutes.
Type 4

Guided Problem-Solving

Goal: solve the problem yourself — AI asks questions, you do the thinking

This is the most demanding type and the most valuable for learning. You are not asking for an explanation or a fix — you are asking AI to help you think. The framing explicitly tells Claude not to solve the problem and to ask you questions instead.

This type takes discipline. AI will want to give you the answer — it is designed to be helpful. Your job is to resist that and keep redirecting: ask me a question, don't solve it for me.

  • Explicitly say: do not solve this — ask me questions instead
  • Tell Claude what you have already tried and where you are stuck
  • After each question Claude asks, answer it fully before asking for the next one
  • If Claude slips and gives you the answer anyway, say: stop — ask me a question instead
Framing pattern 
I want to solve this myself. Do not give me the answer or write any code.

The problem: [DESCRIBE THE PROBLEM]
What I have tried: [WHAT YOU ATTEMPTED]
Where I am stuck: [SPECIFIC STICKING POINT]

Ask me one question at a time to help me figure it out.
Only move to the next question after I answer the current one.
Weak framing
I need to calculate the total value of a portfolio. How do I do it?
Gets the solution immediately. No thinking required. Nothing retained.
Strong framing
I want to calculate the total value of a portfolio. Don't write any code. I think I need a loop, but I'm not sure what to loop over or what to multiply. Ask me one question at a time to help me figure out the structure.
Student works through the logic themselves. Claude guides without solving. Understanding is built, not borrowed.

Take a problem from Lab 1 or Lab 2 that you have already solved. Go back to Claude and use this framing pattern to solve it again from scratch — but this time, guided by questions. Did you understand it differently the second time?

Type 5

Iterative Refinement

Goal: improve something that already works — targeted, not wholesale

You have working code. You want to make it cleaner, faster, more readable, or more robust. The failure mode is asking for a rewrite — which loses your original thinking and produces code you may not understand. Good refinement is surgical, not wholesale.

Always specify what dimension you want to improve. "Make this better" is useless. "Make this more readable for a beginner without changing the logic" is specific and actionable.

  • State what already works so Claude does not change it
  • Name the specific dimension to improve: readability, robustness, simplicity, efficiency
  • Ask for one improvement at a time — not a full rewrite
  • After each change, ask: what did you change and why? Confirm you understand before accepting
Framing pattern 
Here is my working code. Do not rewrite it from scratch.

[PASTE YOUR CODE]

It correctly does: [WHAT IT DOES]
I want to improve specifically: [READABILITY / ROBUSTNESS / SIMPLICITY / OTHER]

Make one targeted change at a time.
After each change, explain what you changed and why.
Weak framing
Here's my portfolio code. Can you improve it?

[pastes code]
Gets a full rewrite with different variable names, different structure, possibly different concepts. Student loses ownership.
Strong framing
Here's my working portfolio function. It correctly calculates total value. I want to make it more readable for a beginner — clearer variable names and maybe a comment explaining the loop. Don't change the logic, don't change the structure. One small improvement at a time.
Gets targeted improvements the student understands and can explain. Ownership preserved.
Type 6

Research & Synthesis

Goal: understand a topic, compare options, or synthesize information

Research questions are where AI tunnel vision is most dangerous. Ask "what is the best data structure for a portfolio?" and Claude will give you one answer, confidently. Ask for a comparison with tradeoffs and you get something you can actually think with.

Always ask for structure — pros and cons, comparison tables, tradeoffs. And always verify: AI can be confidently wrong on factual questions, especially anything involving current tools, libraries, or best practices.

  • Ask for comparisons and tradeoffs, not just a single recommendation
  • Ask: what is the strongest argument against the option you recommended?
  • Ask: what would an expert who disagrees with you say?
  • For factual claims, ask: how confident are you? What should I verify independently?
Framing pattern 
I want to understand [TOPIC] well enough to make my own decision.

Don't just give me the best answer. Give me:
1. A brief overview of the main options or approaches
2. The key tradeoff for each
3. Your recommendation and why
4. The strongest argument against your recommendation

Flag anything I should verify independently.
Weak framing
Should I use a list or a dictionary for my portfolio?
Gets one answer. Student accepts it without understanding why. Can't defend the choice if asked.
Strong framing
I'm deciding between a list of tuples and a list of dictionaries for storing portfolio positions. Give me the key tradeoff for each, your recommendation, and the strongest reason someone might choose the other one. I want to make my own decision.
Student understands both options. Can explain and defend their choice. Deeper learning.
Type 7

Creative & Generative

Goal: generate options, ideas, or original content — not a single fixed answer

Creative questions are where constraints matter most. "Give me ideas for my lab writeup" produces generic suggestions. "Give me five different ways to frame the portfolio problem for a non-technical reader, each using a different analogy" produces something useful.

For generative tasks, always ask for options rather than one answer, give constraints upfront, and use the output as a starting point — not a finished product. The best use of AI for creative work is to generate raw material you then shape yourself.

  • Ask for multiple options, not a single answer — you choose which fits
  • Give constraints upfront: audience, length, tone, what to avoid
  • Treat AI output as a first draft — revise and make it your own
  • Ask: what is a completely different approach to this that you have not suggested yet?
Framing pattern 
I need [TYPE OF CONTENT] for [PURPOSE/AUDIENCE].

Constraints:
- [LENGTH OR FORMAT]
- [TONE OR STYLE]
- [WHAT TO AVOID]

Give me three different versions or approaches — not one.
I will choose and refine from there. Label each clearly.
Weak framing
Write an introduction for my lab report.
Gets one version. Student submits it mostly unchanged. Not their voice, not their thinking.
Strong framing
I need an opening paragraph for my Lab 2 writeup explaining the portfolio system to a non-technical reader. Give me three versions: one using a sports analogy, one using a shopping analogy, one using no analogy at all. Each should be 2–3 sentences. I'll pick one and rewrite it in my own voice.
Student gets options, chooses deliberately, rewrites in their voice. Output is genuinely theirs.

One Habit That Covers All Seven

Every framing pattern in this section shares one underlying move: telling AI what kind of answer you want before you ask the question. That one habit — naming your intent upfront — is the difference between a useful AI interaction and a frustrating one.

The single most useful sentence you can add to any question

Before your question, add one sentence that names what you want: "I want to understand this, not just get the answer." Or: "I just need this to work — skip the explanation." Or: "Give me three options and I'll choose." That one sentence changes everything that follows.

These patterns work everywhere

Use these framing patterns with any AI platform, for any subject, in any context. They are not course-specific. A student who internalizes all seven has a skill that transfers to every class, every job, and every problem they will encounter where AI is a useful tool.

And one pointer for this course specifically

In OIM 3600, the single most important thing you will learn to specify is the data representation — the structure your information lives in. Workbook 3 (Data Structures) makes that concrete, and Prompt 2 makes it a requirement: by mid-semester, naming your structure before any code is written will be the first move of every conversation.

Writing Your Own Code Comments

For the exam, your code must be commented — but every comment must be written by you, not generated by AI. This applies to all four prompts: Claude never adds comments to code it generates. The comments are how you demonstrate understanding.

What a useful comment does

A useful comment explains why a section of code exists, what state it changes, or how it protects an invariant. It does not restate what the line does — the code already shows that.

Strong comment
# total is the state — it accumulates across
# every item in the loop before the discount
# is applied. Must never go below zero.
total = total + (price * qty)
Weak comment
# Add price times qty to total
total = total + (price * qty)

The strong comment names the variable's role in the state, explains its position in the flow, and references an invariant. The weak comment just restates the code in words — which adds no evidence of understanding.

The bottom line

Your pseudocode, your transcript, and your manual comments together tell the full story of how you solved the problem. Each one demonstrates a different layer of understanding — and together they show that the thinking was yours.
